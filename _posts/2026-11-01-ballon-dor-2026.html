One

Three men, three completely different seasons

The award covered the 2025-26 club season and the 2026 World Cup in North America. The three who finished on the podium had almost nothing in common.

73 goals by Kane in 65 matches — the second-biggest scoring season by anyone this century, behind Messi's 82 in 2011-12. Three trophies, and England's best World Cup finish since 1966.
3 scoring charts won by Mbappé: the Pichichi, the Champions League and the World Cup Golden Boot. He finished the season with no medals of any kind.
19 years old. Yamal won La Liga, the Supercopa and the World Cup, led La Liga for assists, and scored once in six World Cup matches.

How strong was each man's league?

UEFA ranks its associations on a rolling five-year coefficient, which is the closest thing football has to an official measure of league strength. By that measure the Premier League is comfortably Europe's strongest and the Bundesliga is only the fourth strongest of the big five.

League strength against votes won
Left: UEFA's five-year association coefficient. Right: Ballon d'Or points won by players from that league. The strongest league produced the fewest points of the three that mattered.
Kane won the Ballon d'Or from the fourth-ranked of Europe's big five leagues. The Premier League, ranked first, put seven men on the shortlist and they collected 1,195 points between them — fewer than Bayern's four players managed on their own.

Four

The United Kingdom question

Four of the hundred jurors represent the home nations. The suspicion that they swung an England captain to the Ballon d'Or is worth testing properly, because the arithmetic is genuinely tight — and the answer has a twist in it.

And now the twist

The home nations did not vote as a bloc. Wales put Lamine Yamal first and Kane second. Paul Abbandonato's ballot is one of only thirteen in the world to lead with Yamal.

Scotland is the outlier, not England. John Greechan's ballot gave Kane fifteen points and Mbappé none at all — the single most pro-Kane ballot on earth. England's juror, Henry Winter, had Mbappé fifth.

Two things this does not show. It is not evidence of bias: a journalist watching a striker score 73 goals may simply rate him highest, as 40 jurors from 39 other countries did. And no single ballot could have changed the result — the largest swing available from one is fifteen points against a twenty-eight point lead.

Five

The podium and the respect zone are two different competitions

A ballot has two halves. The podium is where a juror says who was best. Places four to seven are where a juror says who deserved respect. Almost entirely different players live in each.

Every ballot, split into bands
Each bar is all 100 ballots. Click a band in the legend to remove it.
Kane tops the podium list with 64 and sits ninth in the respect zone with 29. Khvicha Kvaratskhelia is the mirror image: 19 podiums, but 49 appearances between fourth and seventh — more than anyone. That is what sixth place in a Ballon d'Or is made of.

This is where the real geography of the vote shows up, and it is not the one most people assumed. Mbappé was the podium pick of Africa — 65% of CAF jurors put him in their top three against 45% for Kane. Europe was Kane's base — 71% of UEFA jurors had him on the podium against 56% for Mbappé.

Six

Were they rivals, or running mates?

When a juror rated Kane highly, what did that juror do with Mbappé? Two tests give two different answers, and the combination is the finding.

Test one · the podium

Forty jurors found room for both men in their top three. Fisher's exact test on the 2×2 table gives an odds ratio of 0.83 and p = 0.83. Having Kane on your podium told you nothing about whether Mbappé was on it too. The podium was simply wide enough for the pair of them.

Test two · the order

Among the 95 jurors who named both, rating one higher went with rating the other lower. Spearman's rank correlation is −0.25, p = 0.013. Inside the order they were substitutes for the same slot.

Head to head: Europe rated Kane higher 27–14. Africa rated Mbappé higher 14–6. The Americas and Asia were close to even. The 2026 Ballon d'Or was settled by a disagreement between two continents.

Seven

The voting network

Every chart so far has counted things. A network does something different: it treats the hundred ballots as a structure and asks whether jurors cluster into groups that vote alike — and whether those groups follow football's map.

Each ballot is compared with every other using cosine similarity across all thirty players' point allocations. Each juror is then linked to its five nearest neighbours, and Louvain community detection looks for groups in the result.

Who votes like whom
100 jurors, 388 links. Scroll to zoom, drag to pan, hover a dot for the juror and their top five, hover a line for the similarity score. Position comes from ballot similarity, not geography, and a line is a statistical resemblance — not contact between journalists.

Does football's map explain the vote?

If confederation really drove voting, ballots from the same confederation should look more like each other than like ballots from elsewhere. That is testable: compare the average within-group similarity to the between-group average, then shuffle the confederation labels 9,999 times to see how often chance produces a gap that big.

0.728 vs 0.721 average ballot similarity within the same confederation against across confederations. The gap is 0.007.
p = 0.28 Shuffling the labels produces a gap that large about 28% of the time. No overall continental split.
Both things are true at once; let's see how. There is no continental bloc voting across the jury as a whole. But inside the Kane-versus-Mbappé choice specifically, Europe and Africa pulled in opposite directions.

The most ordinary ballot in the world

Neil Davidson of The Globe and Mail in Canada voted most like everyone else, and his ten names line up with the final standings at 0.977.

The least ordinary

Igor Linnyk of FootClub in Ukraine put Fabián Ruiz first, Pau Cubarsí second and Kane ninth, and left out Mbappé and Messi. Unconventional is not the same as wrong: the midfielder who ran PSG's Champions League-winning midfield is a defensible answer.

Voting twins

Across all 4,950 pairs of ballots, the two most alike in the world belong to Poland and New Zealand — Maciej Iwański in Warsaw and Michael Burgess in Auckland, who matched on the top three, shared eight of their ten names and have never met. None of the five closest pairs share a confederation.

The PSG paradox

PSG retained the Champions League, beating Arsenal on penalties in Budapest, and won Ligue 1. They placed nine of the thirty nominees — almost a third of the shortlist. Their players were selected 256 times, more than any other club's. Ninety-one of the hundred ballots named at least two PSG men.

Their best finisher was sixth.

Points won by club, and where PSG's nine finished
The European champions collected 1,326 points from nine players. Bayern collected 1,364 from four.
PSG split their own vote nine ways while Bayern concentrated theirs on a striker who scored 73 goals. It is the strongest argument in the data for why a team award and an individual award measure different things.
Spain won the World Cup, had the most nominees and the most points — and no winner
Points won by the nationality of the nominee, with each nation's World Cup finish. Spain gathered 1,969 points, more than any other nation, from six nominees. England had three nominees, 1,219 points, and the winner. Six Spanish nominees meant six places for Spanish votes to scatter across.

Nine

What-ifs you can run yourself

Two fair questions. Would a different scoring rule have changed the winner? And how few ballots would have had to change? Well, a different rule would probably have produced different ballots as can be seen in the chart below.

Change the scoring rule
The submitted rankings never change; only what each position is worth.
Kane wins on first places alone, on podium-weighted scoring, under the official scale, and by more if the top is steeper. On approval — simply counting who was named — it is 98 to 97. The one rule that changes the answer is the plainest of all: a straight line from ten down to one produces a dead tie at 780 points each.
Remove ballots and watch the result move
Switch jurors off to recount the award without them. Start with a preset, or pick countries yourself. Three ballots removed — Scotland, Austria and Denmark — is enough to flip the award. This measures how close the race was; it is not a suggestion that anything was wrong.

Eleven

Method

What the data is

One hundred ballots, one journalist per country, ten ranked names each, scored 15-12-10-8-7-5-4-3-2-1. Ballots were taken from the El Nacional country-by-country interactive, which attributes them to France Football; snapshot of 1 November 2026.

Methods used

Cosine similarity over each ballot's thirty-player point vector; a five-nearest-neighbour graph built from it; Louvain community detection; betweenness centrality on the bipartite juror-player graph; a permutation test with 9,999 label shuffles; Fisher's exact test on podium co-occurrence; Spearman rank correlation; and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for home bias. The community count depends on the Louvain resolution setting, which yields between four and ten groups across the range tested, so the groups are a lens rather than fixed factions.
